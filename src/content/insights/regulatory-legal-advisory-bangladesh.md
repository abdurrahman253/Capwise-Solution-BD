# Regulatory & Legal Advisory for Businesses in Bangladesh

Businesses operating in Bangladesh sit under multiple regulatory authorities simultaneously — not just one. Depending on structure, sector, and ownership, a company can find itself answering to the RJSC, NBR, Bangladesh Bank, BIDA, sector regulators, and local municipal authorities, often at the same time and with different filing calendars. Regulatory and legal advisory exists to make sense of which of these actually apply to a given business, and to keep it ahead of changes rather than reacting to them after the fact.

## Where Regulatory Complexity Actually Comes From

**Sector-specific licensing.** Import/export, manufacturing, food and pharmaceuticals, financial services, and telecom all carry additional approvals beyond standard company registration — and these obligations exist independently of a company's tax or VAT status.

**Foreign ownership.** Foreign-invested companies generally need BIDA registration and, depending on structure, additional approvals for capital remittance, work permits, and repatriation of profits — layered on top of the standard domestic compliance obligations every company faces.

**A genuinely fast-moving regulatory environment.** Bangladesh's regulatory framework has seen real, substantive change in recent years — VAT thresholds were lowered significantly by a January 2025 ordinance, and labour law underwent its most significant overhaul in two decades through a February 2026 amendment. A business that hasn't reviewed its compliance position against current rules in the last 12–18 months may be operating on assumptions that no longer hold.

**Contractual and commercial risk.** Beyond statutory compliance, day-to-day commercial activity — contracts, vendor agreements, employment terms, dispute exposure — carries its own legal risk that doesn't show up on a compliance checklist but can be just as costly.

## Why "We'll Deal With It If It Comes Up" Doesn't Work Here

Regulatory risk in Bangladesh tends to be invisible until it isn't. A business can operate for years assuming a rule doesn't apply to it, only to discover during a bank facility review, an investor's due diligence process, or a licensing renewal that the assumption was wrong — and that the exposure has been accumulating the whole time, not just from the point it was discovered.

This is particularly true where thresholds are involved — turnover thresholds for VAT, worker-count thresholds for labour obligations, capital thresholds for certain approvals. Crossing a threshold doesn't trigger an automatic notification from the regulator; the obligation to notice and act sits with the business.

## What Proactive Regulatory Advisory Looks Like in Practice

- **Periodic compliance reviews**, not just reactive fixes when a problem surfaces.
- **Tracking regulatory changes** that affect the specific sector and structure a business operates under, rather than general awareness of "the law."
- **Threshold monitoring** — turnover, headcount, capital — since these are the triggers most likely to shift a business's obligations without an external prompt.
- **Contract and commercial risk review**, not just statutory compliance, since legal exposure isn't limited to what a regulator checks.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How often should a business review its regulatory compliance position in Bangladesh?
Given how frequently thresholds and requirements have changed recently, at least annually — and immediately after any major operational change (crossing a turnover or headcount threshold, entering a new sector, taking on foreign investment).

### Does regulatory advisory only matter for large companies?
No — SMEs are often more exposed precisely because they're less likely to have dedicated legal or compliance staff tracking changes, and recent threshold changes (VAT, labour law) have pulled many smaller businesses into scope for the first time.

### What's the risk of not getting regulatory advice?
Exposure generally accumulates from the point an obligation is triggered, not from the point it's discovered — meaning delayed awareness doesn't reduce the exposure, it just delays addressing it.

## Conclusion

Regulatory compliance in Bangladesh isn't a fixed checklist a business completes once — it's a moving target shaped by thresholds, sector rules, and a regulatory environment that has changed substantially even within the past 12 months. Businesses that review their position proactively tend to catch these shifts before they become costly; those that don't tend to find out during due diligence, a licensing renewal, or a dispute.
